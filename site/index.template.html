What the model says

Four findings that fall out of the simulation.

×__MULTI_RATIO__multi-peril years

Dependence changes what can happen, not what usually does

Storms, floods and shrink–swell droughts share a climate. Modelling that properly makes it __MULTI_RATIO__ times more likely that the same home is hit by two or more perils in one year — __MULTI_VINE__% of years against __MULTI_INDEP__% under independence. But both are rare, so a single home's own 1-in-100 loss barely moves: measured at ±0 within simulation noise. The dependence surfaces where it should, in the aggregate — catastrophic years cost __CAT_VS_INDEP__% more than an independence assumption implies.

×__FL_MULT__flood cost, typical → bad

Bad years aren't just "more weather"

Weather is the workhorse loss in every year, but it is the secondary perils that turn a typical year (£__TYPICAL__ per policy) into a bad one (£__BAD__). Going from typical to bad, flood cost multiplies __FL_MULT__× and subsidence __SUB_MULT__×, while weather multiplies just __WX_MULT__×.

TVaRnot VaR

The obvious risk measure gives the wrong answer here

With rare compound losses, tail dependence concentrates claims into fewer, worse years — pushing losses beyond the 99.5% quantile. Value-at-Risk can therefore fall as dependence rises. Premiums use expected shortfall (TVaR 99%), which is monotone in dependence and doesn't hide the clustering.

25%of a home's claims cost

Each peril is pinned to what the market actually paid

Storm, flood and subsidence are calibrated separately to the ABI's 2025 payouts — £244m, £312m and £307m across ~15.5m policies — rather than to a single blended figure. Together they come to £__MEAN_EL__ per policy per year, about a quarter of what all home claims cost. The rest of a real premium is escape of water, theft, fire, accidental damage and expenses, none of which this models. How the calibration works →

100%of open data

Every hazard input is public, and it matches the record

BGS geology, Met Office climatologies, Environment Agency, Natural Resources Wales and SEPA flood maps, EA groundwater alert areas — all Open Government Licence, all fetched by scripts in the repo. Reconstructing 35 years of those drivers picks out 1990, 2015 and 2020 as the worst storm years and 1995, 2018 and 2022 as the driest summers — the Burns' Day storm, Desmond, Ciara and Dennis, and the known subsidence-surge years. This is a demonstration model, not a priced product. The hazard data is real and current, and each peril's loss level is pinned to published ABI payouts — but these are four-peril technical premiums, not home insurance premiums. A real policy also covers escape of water, theft, fire and accidental damage, and carries expenses, commission and profit on top. The caveats section is explicit about every assumption.
