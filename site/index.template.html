Eight insured perils.
2,736 postcode districts.
One dependence model.

A UK home-insurance risk model that scores every postcode district for subsidence, weather damage, flood, groundwater flooding, theft, escape of water, fire and accidental damage — the weather-driven four joined by a vine copula, because the expensive years are the ones where several perils strike at once; the other four priced independently, because burglary does not arrive on the storm track, burst pipes answer to cold snaps, and kitchen fires and dropped televisions pay no attention to the sky at all. Coastal erosion is modelled alongside them but kept out of the premium, because no standard policy covers it.

×__MULTI_RATIO__multi-peril years

Dependence changes what can happen, not what usually does

Storms, floods and shrink–swell droughts share a climate. Modelling that properly makes it __MULTI_RATIO__ times more likely that the same home is hit by two or more perils in one year — __MULTI_VINE__% of years against __MULTI_INDEP__% under independence. But both are rare, so a single home's own 1-in-100 loss barely moves: measured at ±0 within simulation noise. The dependence surfaces where it should, in the aggregate — catastrophic years cost __CAT_VS_INDEP__% more than an independence assumption implies.

×__FL_MULT__flood cost, typical → bad

Bad years aren't just "more weather"

Weather is the workhorse loss in every year, but it is the secondary perils that turn a typical year (£__TYPICAL__ per policy) into a bad one (£__BAD__). Going from typical to bad, flood cost multiplies __FL_MULT__× and subsidence __SUB_MULT__×, while weather multiplies just __WX_MULT__×.

TVaRnot VaR

The obvious risk measure gives the wrong answer here

With rare compound losses, tail dependence concentrates claims into fewer, worse years — pushing losses beyond the 99.5% quantile. Value-at-Risk can therefore fall as dependence rises. Premiums use expected shortfall (TVaR 99%), which is monotone in dependence and doesn't hide the clustering.

__EL_CLAIMS_SHARE__%of a home's claims cost

Each peril is pinned to what the market actually paid

Storm, flood and subsidence are calibrated separately to the ABI's 2025 payouts — £__CAL_WX_PAID__m, £__CAL_FL_PAID__m and £__CAL_SUB_PAID__m across ~15.5m policies — theft to the floor of its documented vintage envelope (£__CAL_TH_PAID__m), escape of water to the ABI's £1.8m-a-day figure (~£__CAL_EOW_PAID__m a year), fire to a documented triangle of Home Office incident counts and the ABI's average fire payout (~£__CAL_FIRE_PAID__m a year), and accidental damage to a triangle of its own — the ABI's claim count, the market's at-home AD share and Aviva's average payout (~£__CAL_AD_PAID__m a year) — rather than to a single blended number. Together the eight insured perils come to £__MEAN_EL__ per policy per year, __EL_CLAIMS_SHARE__% of what all home claims cost. The rest of a real premium is expenses — which this does not model — plus commission and profit. How the calibration works →

100%of open data

Every hazard input is public, and it matches the record

BGS geology, Met Office climatologies, Environment Agency, Natural Resources Wales and SEPA flood maps, EA groundwater alert areas — all Open Government Licence, all fetched by scripts in the repo. Reconstructing 35 years of those drivers picks out 1990, 2015 and 2020 as the worst storm years and 1995, 2018 and 2022 as the driest summers — the Burns' Day storm, Desmond, Ciara and Dennis, and the known subsidence-surge years.
